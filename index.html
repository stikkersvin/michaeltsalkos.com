Essay + Poster 2025 novThe Gram as Personal BrandingSelf-initiatedEssay, Poster Description The Gram as Personal Branding examines how identity and personal branding are constructed through social media. Details Year:
2025 nov Client:
Self-initiated Format:
Essay, Poster Designer:
Michael Tsalkos Writer:
Michael Tsalkos IG is a great tool to showcase work, document processes and to catch a profile’s sense of visual style. In my own experience with posting my own work on the platform for over five years, I’ve experienced a lot of benefits, but also some aspects I’m more critical of when it comes to shaping one’s online presence. @student studios It seems that in this day and age, having a profile is a must, whether it’s a personal or professional profile. Maybe you like to keep them separate? Many people do. But I can’t help noticing that during my studies, a lot of fellow students have started these so-called “studio” profiles, even though they don’t have a physical studio and work as a single person. But I guess people just think it sounds cool. These profiles are often active for a short period of time and then abandoned, left to wonder in the digital abyss, a scrapyard of old projects, a page never to be updated again. But why though? Perhaps because there’s this expectation to perform well, and when the posts don’t do, it’s somehow embarrassing. Or maybe it could just be because of laziness, the profiles losing patience with posting, making stories, and keeping it all up for a small audience. And honestly, both being embarrassed or being impatient makes sense. We crave fast results, in likes and comments, as a simple form of validation, it’s a proof that something’s good, if gets plenty of thumbs up. But not getting a ton of likes on a poster, does not mean a bad project. What’s rarely considered is how much the algorithm affects visibility. Factors like: time of day you post, the type of content, and how many likes it gets in the first ten minutes can all play a role. I don’t know all the details, but from experience (and some light research), I do know that posts with faces, selfies, photos from nights out, vacation pictures perform way better than graphics. So maybe the trick is to post a selfie with your poster next time. Web or Gram Still, many designers care deeply about keeping a strict separation between their graphic design account and their personal one. For years I’ve been wondering if these two universes could merge and for a long time I didn’t think it was possible, and potential clients might find it odd to scroll through posters and personal archiving. It feels contradictory. You wouldn’t see that contradiction on a portfolio website. And if you were to see the same content from instagram on a website it would be considered unprofessional, but in reverse it would be fine. Even though both platforms could serve similar functions. Yet, Instagram seems far more important than a website. With its recommendation features and algorithmic visibility, it’s much easier to discover like-minded profiles, related work and find potential jobs. Some time ago, we visited the Copenhagen office of the studio Åbake. What fascinated me before going there was actually their lack of online presence. They barely exist online, aside from a very minimal website that doesn’t say much about them. Work Life Balance Which is quite interesting, considering they’ve worked with clients like Virgil Abloh, Balenciaga, and others. But it’s also worth remembering that the founders started out in the early 2000s, when having a website wasn’t as crucial and word of mouth carried more weight. For them, not having a proper webpage or instagram is a statement. It shows great confidence, but at the same time huge privilege. They’re established enough to not need an online presence, which for designers of today, would be impossible to do. We rely on being visible and easily accessible across platforms. For most people, it’s easier to discover someone through Instagram than through a website. The app’s structure makes it more approachable, easier to manage as a single user, and more intuitive to navigate since everyone’s familiar with the interface. In contrast to Åbake and the student studio instagram, I also recently came across Charlotte Rohde, a Berlin-based graphic and type designer whose work I find really inspiring. What I admire is not only her graphics, but how she presents both herself and her work as one cohesive whole on instagram. Her personality and visual language melt together, her presence becomes part of her design practice, showing that you can move fluidly between the personal and the professional. So it’s not impossible to do! Going back to the student studio thing, I also think there’s something very interesting with how people curate their bios, some people won’t showcase their work, but will flex their position at an agency or which school they attend. It’s almost like a form of subtle signaling in the form of a digital badge of belonging. Like kinda a tribal marker, indicating which community, institution, or aesthetic a person identifies with. In the end, I think it’s about balance. Platforms like instagram give us a voice and visibility that designers before didn’t have, but they also come with an invisible pressure to perform, to label ourselves, to belong. Whether we choose to post, to curate, or to step back entirely, we’re still participating in a system that constantly shapes how we see each other and ourselves. 2025The Values As A Miracle / Photosynthesis

New Edge 666 by Charlotte Rohde
Sneaky Times by Jules Durand We, the undersigned, are graphic designers, photographers, and students who have been brought up in a world in which the techniques and apparatus of creativity have persistently been presented to us as the most lucrative, effective and desirable means of using our talents. You might have read this very well known sentence before. Maybe you stumbled over it in a newspaper, a magazine, on the web, have had a lecture about it or have had a conversation with a lecturer about it. You might have also not heard about it, I didn’t either up until very recently. In 1964, British graphic designer Ken Garland wrote the infamous original manifesto which he named ‘‘first things first’’, a manifesto that argues what we as visual communicators should prioritize first in our field, which at the time was a belief that we should use our power and design skills to form a better future, rather than focusing on irrelevant advertisment graphics, in the form of products like cat food, detergent, slip ons and toothpaste. He acknowledges that these products still need to be made, but we (the designers) should prioritize it as a secondary objective. At the time the text was signed by 20 designers working at the time. (An overview and summaries of the various versions of the manifesto can be read on page five). The manifest got recognition, and later was published in the The Guardian and it got further reach. Later on, designers took the format and used it as a base to speak up about the struggles of our field in different times since 1964. If you haven’t read the different versions before, I would highly recommend you to do so, every point from each version still stands to this day and the versions can be read on the internet. The different versions touch on anti consumerism, the environment and survelliance. This version is something else. This is meant as an uproar and oppurtunity to take a look inside ourselves and recognize that soon, we as individuals and as a group with a profession, in an international community are about to get absolutely fucked. Today the techniques and apparatus have evolved and the way that we recklessly use them without thinking of consequences, might contributes to our demise. If this is to continue we might soon experience the death of our field. Who it this perputrator? I’m of course talking about our companions which come in the form of Monica, Claude or mostly referred to as Chatgpt or Chat. Constantly there’s this fear that Chat will do the job for us, in such a way that is cheaper, faster, colder. We talk about our jobs being important, but with each new update and each new model, we are reminded our practice may very soon be rendered obsolete. Yet despite this awareness, we very quietly still contribute to the extinction of other alike professions. We would rather generate a drawn image than having the illustration drawn by hand or have a fake video made rather than having a real video filmed. We are the contributors of the destruction of other fields and if that is so, then why should others care about our work if we don’t care about their work? I do recognize that artificial intelligence is a tool, we experiment with it, we explore and we adapt. But at the same time we should also refuse to hop where the fence is lowest and it is about time that we should be critical towards these technologies and companies, for what they do or rather what they do against the future. We should be critical towards the authenticity, both in what they offer for information, but also how it contributes towards the authenticity that we produce as creatives. How it takes a lot of different references and creates something that is mediocre, unoriginal and seen before. I encourage you to say no to these tools, and if you can afford collaboration choose collaboration. If you can commission, then commission and if you can sustain human work, sustain it. Some of you who are reading this might be freelancing, others might be working at a studio or a big bureau, where things are easier said than done. But even though you work for higher powers, it is still important to at least have the conversation with peers or people around you, maybe protest and maybe consider your position and stance, if they don’t align with your values. Then what? That’s the scary part, but if it wasn’t scary, it would mean that it wasn’t important. There’s still many possibilities and opportunities, and if you can rely on your skills, and get a job based on your skills in the first place, it means that you can probably impress somebody to get a new position again. Or take the step to create something of your own, and people would appreciate that, that there are more studios or freelancers who actually care about creativity, and in the end you would probably also attract the companies or people you align your values with, but also create the work that you actually want to do. I am not saying not to use ai, some might think that you will be behind in the job market if it’s a skill you haven’t ‘’learned’’. But at least understand it and what it does. In the end, it won’t ever be able to create the imperfections, the randomness and the tactility that we as designers have studied for years and have learned to master through experimentation and time. Experimentation in an analogue context, in a tech context but also in a written context. This whole text is written, probably with a lot of errors, spelling mistakes and other grammatical problems. The language (and graphics for that sake) is deliberately in a writing style that is me, talking to you, in the most informal style. But that is also the beauty of it, just like the graphics. That this is not polished, and has not been run through a large language model, which tends to take your work, thoughts, ideas and minimizes it to something that it sees as better, but is it actually better? In the end text ‘‘optimized’’ by Chat is more plain and in the end boring, it’s easily spottable — especially when it uses an em dash, and a ‘‘smart’’ remark to put emphasis on a point. The only thing it does, is that it makes the job faster. But faster does not mean quality. We live in a world, where everything is faster and faster everyday, but some things need to go at their own pace, it needs to have time to play with and explore, the thing that made us choose the path of design in the first place and made us fall in love with it. Theres something facinating and human in doing a document, choosing size, color, font, shape, aesthetic, function, references, inspiration, reshaping, making a new version, and another one and then another one, evaluating, getting back from a break with new ideas and concepts, executing, saving, choosing paper, printing method, deciding how and finally where it is being displayed. All of theese decisions are not being considered as ‘‘fast’’ and all of these decisions are not up to you when doing a prompt. Faster for the ai means less care, and why design if you don’t care, if there’s no passion for what you are producing, why even produce? As a designer, which I still feel weird addressing myself as, I recognize that it’s a really huge privilege to be in a position where I can study the field of design, get paid to study it and get paid to do work for others. Now when you take this privilege, and use ai as a tool, it’s like treating your own work like shit, like it’s worthless. Also at the same time, when taking other designers’ style and feeding the chat with it, which means you are stealing others work and that their work is beneath appreciation and that it’s worthless. Just because you wanted something that was faster and cheaper, which is interesting because in every other context those two words in a sentence, in the terms of product, means you’re getting a really bad product that is almost ready to be thrown out. Chatgpt probably won’t disappear and I’m not old enough to remember when Photoshop, inkjet or any other graphic production means got released. But I imagine this technological evolution of artificial intelligence, being the equivalent to when the handheld calculator came out. Back in the day, we were told that it was a must to learn how to do math inside your mind, that it was a necessity. Then the smartphone got released, with the ability to download and access applications, including a pocket calculator and that concept was devalued, but not entirely. There’s still a skill to do it, to do it without the help of technological needs, that will be much more appreciated in the end. Which will put you in an advantage. Just like the art of writing, thinking, brainstorming and coming up with new ideas. You could try to make Chat generate ideas for you, but if you have ever tried that before, it seems like it’s stuck on trying to come up with content that thinks outside the box. So what can you do? And what do I propose for you to undersign at the end of the document? This is exactly the part I want you to agree on. I want you to take these reflections, make your own and be aware of the contributions that we are making to the damage of the earth and creativity. I encourage you to read and understand the issue better, to talk to peers about the subject and reflect and question, if the action of using Chat is absolutely necessary for the task you are performing. Take inspiration from the 30% rule, use your creativity and reject the slop when spotted. Don’t support companies devaluating a human worker to something redundant that is being replaced by a machine. And I repeat again, If you can commission, commission and if you can sustain human work, sustain it! 1964:
